# Connectivity of a uniform random inversion graph

In §6 of his November 12 preprint, Emil Verkama asks whether a uniform permutation has an inversion graph whose vertex connectivity equals its minimum degree with probability tending to one. Vertices are positions, and two positions are adjacent if their entries occur in decreasing order. Connectivity is the least number of vertices whose deletion disconnects the graph, with the usual complete-graph convention. Proving equality with high probability would give the Rayleigh limiting distribution of connectivity from Bhattacharya and Mukherjee’s theorem for minimum degree. This is a question about all permutation orders as their size tends to infinity. [Verkama, §6, “Connectivity as a statistic”](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17237v1#S6) · [Minimum-degree theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03582)

The approach is to represent a cut by a pair of position and value thresholds. A small vertex cut must be near a diagonal corner. A non-singleton component behind a cut of size at most the minimum degree would force two permutation points to lie unusually close in both coordinates.

## Full Lean source theorem

The declaration `RandomInversionConnectivity.verkama_connectivity : VerkamaQuestion` proves that the finite proportion of permutations with equal vertex connectivity and minimum degree tends to one. The source theorem has no mathematical hypotheses. The sharp $1/n$ asymptotic and the Rayleigh consequence are additional written results. [Full theorem](../../lean/SolvedMath/RandomInversionProbability.lean) · [Sample space and graph conventions](../../lean/SolvedMath/RandomInversionConnectivity.lean)

For the probabilistic part Lean counts prescribed assignments in a uniform permutation and derives that the variance of the number of points in a rectangle is at most its mean. Its four finite tests are an occupied corner, enough points crossing any threshold away from the corner, a limit on the corner population, and the absence of a close pair there. The same tests on the reversed diagram handle the other corner. For each positive integer $t$ the proof constructs a size threshold beyond which the failure probability is at most $14/t$. This proves convergence using only first and second moments. The sharper rate in the manuscript uses stronger tail estimates.
