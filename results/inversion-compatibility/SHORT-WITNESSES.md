# The finite search in Table 2 suffices at every length

**Theorem.** If an individual pattern $p$ of length $h$ is incompatible with the prescribed injection, there is a witness of length between $h-1$ and $h+2$. Both the input and its image are actual 1324-avoiders. The additive upper bound is sharp. For multiple forbidden patterns the separate finite-basis compression theorem preserves avoidance of *all* input restrictions. [Classification](CLASSIFICATION.md) · [Finite-basis theorem](PROOF.md) · [Original search window](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01143v2#page=29)

The lower bound follows from $|f(w)|=|w|+1$. The maximum-leading and late second-largest constructions in the classification use at most $h+2$ points. A decomposable witness needs only $h-1$. We give witnesses for all the regular cases here. They also verify sufficiency of Test 4 independently of the construction in source Theorem 7.9. An input defined in normalized coordinates is returned to its original orientation before applying the prescribed map.

## A decomposable tail

Write $q$ for the normalized pattern and $r=q_1<h$. Suppose $c(q)\le2$ and Test 4 succeeds. First suppose the tail has a cut and write it as $A\oplus T$ with $T$ its last indecomposable component and $s=|A|\ge1$. Then $T$ avoids 213 and $A$ is an indecomposable 132-avoiding component followed by zero or more singletons. When $q$ is indecomposable, its first entry is above $A$: otherwise the direct cut after $A$ would survive inserting the first entry. In particular $r\ge s+2$ in this case.

If $q$ is decomposable with two components $D\oplus E$, each regular test with a cut tail gives $D$ beginning at its maximum. For an increased component count the deletion of its first point splits $D$. A nonsingleton indecomposable 132-avoider splits as $U\ominus V$ with the maximum at the end of $U$; if the first point is below the maximum, both sides survive its deletion, contradicting a split. The second test already asks for $D$ to begin at its maximum. For the 213-avoiding-tail test, $r>1$ implies $D$ is nonsingleton. Then $D$ minus its first entry is increasing, since every component before the last in a 213-avoider is a singleton. If the maximum of $D$ survived the deletion it would end $D$, contrary to indecomposability. In every case $T=E$, and $A$ has length $s=r-1$.

Use the source word with first point $X$ of one-based rank $r+2$ and tail $A\oplus(T\ominus1)$. Its length is $h+1$. The first deletion succeeds. The pivot is above all of $A$, above the appended minimum of the last block, and above exactly $r-s\ge1$ genuine entries of $T$. This makes the input indecomposable. Its tail avoids 1324 by the two-end description, and a 1324 beginning at $X$ would require a 213 among the higher entries of the final block. Any occurrence using $X$ would need $h-r$ higher entries, yet the input has just $h-r-1$. Omitting $X$ forces the entire length-$h$ tail. This tail decomposes, ruling out indecomposable $q$. In the two-component case its last component has size $|T|+1$ whereas the last component of $q$ has size $|T|$.

Under growth, the new middle point and the appended minimum can be omitted. The crossed point was in the designated $T$, so the remaining word on $X,A,T$ has first rank $r$ and equals $q$. For a late orientation $r\le h-2$, so at least one point of $T$ is above $X$. Deleting the appended last point leaves the connected $T$ with at least one old low. Deleting the physical maximum leaves the appended minimum joined to every surviving point of $T$. Both surviving blocks are connected to $X$, as are all points of $A$. These competing deletions fail. An early orientation needs only the successful first deletion.

## An indecomposable tail

When $T=q\setminus q_1$ is indecomposable, the only regular test left is $1<r<h$ and $T$ avoids 213. Then $q$ is indecomposable: a proper cut would survive deleting its first point since $r>1$. Take tail $1\oplus(T\ominus1)$ and give $X$ rank $r+3$. This input has $h+2$ entries. The extra minimum and the first singleton are below $X$, together with exactly $r$ points of the designated $T$. The first deletion succeeds, the high entries avoid 213, and growth recovers $q$ on $X,T$. In a late orientation the last and maximum deletions fail for the same reasons as above. At least one of the $r$ old lows in the connected $T$ survives either deletion.

Once again an occurrence starting at $X$ would require one more high point than the entire input has. An occurrence omitting $X$ would have to fill the entire block $T\ominus1$ since $q$ is indecomposable. If $q=T\ominus1$, both its first and its last length-$(h-1)$ windows would standardize to $T$. Hence each adjacent comparison in $q$ equals the next one. The word is monotone and ends at its minimum, so it must start at its maximum, contrary to $r<h$. This proves the uniform bound and finishes the sufficiency proof for the singleton classification. ∎
