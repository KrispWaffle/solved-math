# A polynomial-time compatibility criterion

Let $f$ be the precise Linusson–Verkama injection, including the priority convention in §7 of the source. Let $B$ be a finite set of classical permutation patterns. A witness consists of a decomposable or almost decomposable $\pi\in\operatorname{Av}(1324,B)$ and an occurrence in $f(\pi)$ of some $p\in B$.

**Theorem.** If a witness exists, there is a witness $w$ for the same forbidden member $p$ such that $|w|\le |p|+18$. Moreover $w$ contains the standardization of $p$ after deleting at most two of its entries. Consequently compatibility of finite bases can be decided in deterministic polynomial time in their total input length. The constant bounds the extra entries for all choices of patterns, bases and original hosts. [Definition 7.4 and Table 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01143v2#page=27)

## Auxiliary facts about the end components

Write $I_m$ for an increasing block of length $m$. Every decomposable 1324-avoider has the unique form $\sigma\oplus I_m\oplus\tau$, where the end components are nonempty and indecomposable, $\sigma$ avoids 132 and $\tau$ avoids 213. Each nonsingleton indecomposable 132-avoider is a skew sum of two nonempty parts: its maximum separates its smaller suffix from its larger left part, including the maximum. The reverse-complement statement for a nonsingleton indecomposable 213-avoider gives a split $U\ominus V$, where $U$ contains the maximum of the word and $V$ begins at the minimum and contains its last position. This follows by applying the maximum split to the reverse-complement. Taking at least one point from each side of either skew split guarantees that every restriction containing these two points is indecomposable. A singleton needs just its one point. Thus any prescribed subset in such an end component can be enlarged with at most two points so that it stays indecomposable under all further enlargements inside the component. [Source component lemma](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01143v2#page=6)

A 213-avoider $T$ is an initial string of singleton direct-sum components followed by its last component $K$. An inversion in an earlier component and any point from a later component would form 213. We use the description also for an identity by taking its last singleton for $K$.

## Keeping the output occurrence

For decomposable $\pi$, the injection simply increases the middle length by one. Mark the entries of $\pi$ corresponding to the chosen output occurrence, excluding the newly added singleton. Retain up to two additional points from each old end to ensure that both restricted ends stay nonempty and indecomposable. The compressed input has the same component description, and its image realizes the retained occurrence, using its new singleton if needed. The input is a pattern of $\pi$, so it still avoids all of $B$ and 1324. Up to four points beyond the occurrence have sufficed.

For an indecomposable input, one of its boundary deletions makes it decomposable. The boundary choices are the first position, minimum value, last position and maximum value. Use inverse or reverse-complement to normalize the boundary actually used to deletion of the first position. Apply the same symmetry to the witness pattern and all of $B$. We keep the transformed priority from the original map throughout. Write the old word after deleting its first entry $X$ as $\sigma\oplus I_m\oplus\tau$, and let $a=|\sigma|$, $b=|\tau|$.

A cut survives the insertion of an initial entry of rank $r$ precisely when the old initial segment of length $j$ was a proper cut (allowing zero) and $r\le j+1$. The last old proper cut has length $a+m$. Thus indecomposability says that $X$ is above the left component, all middle points, and exactly $v$ entries of $\tau$, where $1\le v\le b$. Call these $v$ entries low. Let $C$ be the largest low value of $\tau$. The image has one additional middle point and keeps the first rank $r=a+m+v+1$. In this image $X$ is above $v-1$ entries of $\tau$. On the old points the only changed comparison is between $X$ and $C$. The new middle point is below $X$ and below all of $\tau$.

Mark the old points in the chosen occurrence other than the new middle point. Keep $X,C$, and keep up to two points in $\sigma$ to preserve its indecomposability. Keep enough points of $\tau$ for its restriction to be indecomposable as well. This preserves indecomposability of the full compressed input: $X$ crosses the last old proper cut because the low point $C$ survives. When its first position is deleted, the compressed word gives the same two end components and its retained middle points. Under the injection, the new rank crosses exactly the retained $C$, which remains the largest retained low point, so the output still contains the chosen pattern. The only issue left is to ensure that the original priority selects this boundary.

## Preserving the priority

The first-position and minimum deletions cannot both make an indecomposable 1324-avoider decomposable. The same fact holds for last-position and maximum deletions. This is Proposition 2.4 in the revised article, cited as Proposition 7 in the subclasses paper. If the chosen boundary was originally first position or minimum, the preceding construction suffices. For an originally last-position or maximum boundary, the normalized first deletion must succeed while the normalized last and maximum deletions must both leave an indecomposable word. The latter two tests correspond to the original first and minimum, possibly interchanged. Inputs with fewer than four entries already satisfy the size bound. [Priority and its symmetry](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01143v2#page=27) · [Proposition 7](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15075)

In this case $X$ cannot be the maximum because deleting it succeeds. The last point $Y$ and maximum $Z$ therefore belong to $\tau$. In particular $v<b$, $Z$ is high, and $b\ge2$. Apply the split $\tau=U\ominus V$ described above, with $Z\in U$ and $Y\in V$. In the inversion graph, every point of $U$ is adjacent to every point of $V$. The other points of the old word outside $\tau$ all lie below $X$. Hence, after deleting $W=Y$ or $W=Z$, indecomposability is equivalent to each nonempty inversion-graph component of $\tau-W$ containing a low vertex. Indeed, $X$ is adjacent to every point of $\sigma$ and the middle, and among $\tau$ it is adjacent exactly to the lows. There are no edges from $\tau$ to $\sigma$ or the middle. Direct-sum components coincide with inversion-graph components.

Keep $Y,Z$ themselves, so they remain the corresponding boundary points after restriction. From each of $U,V$, keep at least two points if it has two, and otherwise keep the singleton. For each $W\in\{Y,Z\}$, keep one low vertex in $\tau-W$ whenever nonempty. It exists because the original deletion was required to leave a connected graph. After either deletion, if both split sides remain, the restricted graph on $\tau-W$ is connected by the skew split and contains the chosen low vertex.

If one side vanishes under a deletion, it was already a singleton in the original. The remaining side $T$ is a 213-avoider, so $T=I_j\oplus K$ with $K$ its nonempty last component. Because every component of the original $T$ contained a low point, all the initial singletons are low and $K$ contains a low point. Retain one such point in $K$ and at most two points to ensure that the restricted $K$ is indecomposable. Every retained component of $T$ now contains a low vertex. This argument remains true if other marked points are added. Make this choice for each singleton-side deletion that occurs. Both required earlier boundary tests therefore still fail. The normalized first-deletion rule is the one applied to the compressed input.

The number of points used in addition to those taken from the output occurrence is at most 18: the two distinguished points $X,C$, two end anchors in $\sigma$, the two boundary points $Y,Z$, up to four points across $U,V$, up to two retained lows for the deletion tests, and up to three further points for each of the two possible single-side cases. The $U,V$ anchors already preserve the indecomposability of $\tau$ in this construction. In the cases with no earlier deletion tests, at most two anchors for $\tau$ suffice. All inputs are subpatterns of the original input, and undoing the normalization preserves containment and length.

Finally, in both forms the original and the image agree on the retained occurrence after removing the new middle entry and the pivot $X$ if those were used. Thus the compressed input itself contains the pattern obtained by deleting at most two entries from $p$. This proves the witness statement.

## An exact recognition algorithm

For each $p\in B$, delete zero, one or two of its entries and standardize. From each resulting pattern generate all permutations obtained by inserting at most 20 new points, in arbitrary position and value order, stopping at length $|p|+18$. This includes every compressed witness by the theorem. If $h$ is the maximum forbidden length, there are at most $(h+1)^2$ choices of deleted positions up to a constant factor, and each insertion uses at most $(h+19)^2$ choices. Twenty nested insertions give a polynomial bound for the total number of candidates. Repetitions can be retained.

For each candidate test 1324-avoidance and the actual boundary priorities directly. Keep exactly decomposable or almost decomposable words. Test avoidance of every member of $B$ in the candidate, construct its image under the prescribed injection, and test whether that image contains $p$. Every reported failure is a witness by definition. The bounded-witness theorem gives the converse. The empty set is compatible; a basis containing the empty pattern has no inputs.

The remaining complexity issue is testing short forbidden patterns in a long candidate. Each retained candidate is a separable permutation after deletion of at most one entry. Indeed, each end avoids 132 or 213, hence avoids both 2413 and 3142 and is separable. Alternatively their recursive maximum or minimum split constructs their direct/skew trees explicitly. Direct sums preserve separability. This also applies to the image by deleting its distinguished pivot, if present.

For a separable text, build its binary tree of direct and skew sums by finding cuts recursively. For every node and contiguous segment of target positions record whether that segment embeds there, with empty segments permitted. At an internal node split the segment at every possible position. Require embedding of the two pieces in the two children and require all target values from the first piece to be below those in the second for a direct node, and above them for a skew node. A leaf can realize an empty or a permitted singleton. Induction proves this algorithm, including with position-dependent permitted text values at leaves. There are polynomially many states and splits.

If deletion of one text point makes it separable, first test embeddings omitting the distinguished point. For embeddings using it, try each target position as its partner. On the remaining target positions retain the comparisons to the distinguished point in both position and value order as unary leaf constraints. The same dynamic program then tests the remaining embedding, and the constraints precisely permit reinsertion of the distinguished point. This gives a polynomial recognition algorithm even when the members of $B$ have different lengths. ∎
