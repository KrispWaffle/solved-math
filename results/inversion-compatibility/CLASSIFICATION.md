# The compatible singleton patterns

For a permutation $p$, compatibility means that the prioritized Linusson–Verkama injection preserves avoidance of $p$ on every decomposable or almost decomposable member of $\operatorname{Av}(1324,p)$. Theorem 7.6 of Claesson, Linusson, Ulfarsson and Verkama determines the asymptotic density of compatible patterns. The following criterion decides each individual pattern directly from its entries. [Source definition and bounds](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01143v2#page=28)

Let $h=|p|$. A pattern containing 1324 is compatible, since the whole image avoids 1324. The empty pattern and the one-point pattern are compatible. For all other patterns let $c(w)$ be the number of nonempty indecomposable direct-sum components of $w$. Always standardize after deleting entries. Use the four **indexed** orientations $q=p,p^{-1},p^{rc},(p^{rc})^{-1}$ in that order. The first two are early and the last two late, even if two of the words are equal. Write $r=q_1$ and $a=q\setminus q_1$.

**Classification.** A pattern $p\in\operatorname{Av}_h(1324)$ with $h\ge2$ is incompatible if and only if one of the following tests succeeds.

1. $c(p)\ge3$.
2. In some orientation $r=h$ and $c(a)\ge3$.
3. In some late orientation $r=h-1$ and one of the following holds:
   - $c(a)\ge3$.
   - $a=A\oplus T$ has exactly two components and $T$ starts below its maximum.
   - $a=T$ is indecomposable, avoids 213, and starts below its maximum.
4. In at least one remaining indexed orientation, $c(a)>c(q)$, or $r>1$ and $q$ has two components with its first component starting at its maximum, or $1<r<h$ and $a$ avoids 213.

The tests refer only to the given pattern. Direct cuts and the four orientations use elementary scans. For 1324, scan inversion pairs and test for a smaller point on the left and a larger point on the right using prefix minima and suffix maxima. For 213, a prior inversion and a sufficiently large suffix maximum suffice. The entire test takes quadratic time and linear auxiliary space. None of the tests requires listing possible input hosts. [Executable criterion](decision.py)

For Test 1, deleting a middle singleton from $p$ gives a decomposable witness. An occurrence newly introduced when a decomposable host grows must use the middle and both ends, so it has at least three components. The same applies to an occurrence omitting the pivot of an almost decomposable host. The component count is invariant under both symmetries. For an almost decomposable host normalize the actual boundary used to deletion of the first point and carry the original priority with it. The necessity argument of Theorem 7.8 applied to the actual chosen orientation gives Test 1 or Test 4 outside the two exceptional ranks. A late regular orientation has rank at most $h-2$. Explicit witnesses prove sufficiency of every regular test under the prescribed priority. [Regular constructions and correction to equation (8)](SHORT-WITNESSES.md) The maximum-leading case in Test 2 is proved separately. The two short arguments below give regular necessity directly and settle Test 3. [Published criteria](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01143v2#page=30) · [Maximum-leading proof](FIRST-MAXIMUM.md)

## The second-largest first entry under late priority

In the normalized source the first point is $X$, and its deletion leaves $S\oplus I_m\oplus J$, with $S,J$ nonempty and indecomposable, $S$ avoiding 132 and $J$ avoiding 213. The pivot is above $S$, every middle point, and the smallest $v\ge1$ values of $J$. Write $C$ for the largest of these $v$ values. The image grows the middle and passes $C$ across $X$. Within the old points only this comparison changes. A late source has at least one genuine high in $J$, meaning a point already above $X$ in the input. Otherwise $X$ was the maximum and the competing maximum deletion would have succeeded. [Domain and priority proof](PROOF.md)

**Regular necessity.** Suppose $c(q)\le2$ and $r<h$. An occurrence newly created by the move must use $X$, and its selected higher point lies in $J$. If it uses no $S$, the selected tail lies in $I_{m+1}\oplus J$, which avoids 213. If $r=1$, substituting any old point from $S$ for $X$ gives the same occurrence in the input, since then the entire selected tail lies in $J$. Thus $1<r<h$ in this case. If $S$, the middle and $J$ are all used, the tail has at least three components, more than $q$. Finally suppose $S,J$ are used and the middle is omitted. The tail has a cut between these two sets of points. An indecomposable $q$, or a tail with more than two components, gives the increased-component test. In the remaining case both $q$ and its tail have two components. Since $X$ lies above the selected points of $S$, prepending it cannot create a cut immediately after itself. The unique tail cut must therefore survive. This places $X$ above the rest of the first component and below the second, giving Test 4. This is the orientation-specific necessity argument behind Theorem 7.8.

**Necessity.** Suppose $c(a)\le2$ and consider a newly present $q$. Its first point has rank $h-1$, so $q$ has at most two components. Consequently its occurrence uses $X$. Its unique higher selected point $H$ is in $J$. If $H\ne C$, the occurrence omits $C$, and its old comparisons agree with those in the input. It must then use the new middle point. If it also uses $S$, the selected tail sees three nonempty direct-sum blocks, contrary to $c(a)\le2$. Otherwise use any point of $S$ for the earliest selected middle point and take the rest from the old middle. This would already give $q$. Thus $H=C$.

The last component $T$ of $a$ contains its maximum $C$ and lies entirely in $J$, since an indecomposable occurrence cannot cross a direct cut. If $T$ starts at $C$, take any genuine high $Z$. Every selected point $L$ of $T$ after $C$ must come after $Z$. Otherwise $C,L,Z$ in this order would form 213 in $J$. In the input $Z$ can therefore replace $C$ at the head of $T$, with all remaining points of $T$ kept. For two tail components the preceding component $A$ lies entirely outside $J$ or entirely inside it. In the latter case any inversion of $A$, followed by $C$, would form 213, so $A$ is a singleton and can be taken from $S$. A singleton using the new middle can also be taken from $S$. In all other cases keep the old realization of $A$. This realizes $q$ in the input, a contradiction. Thus $T$ starts below its maximum. For one component its entire occurrence lies in $J$, so it also avoids 213. This proves necessity, including $h=2$.

**Constructions.** In each of the following inputs start with $X$ of second-largest rank and the indicated standardized tail. The unique point above $X$ is $Z$, the first and largest point of the final skew block. The ends and the increasing middle ensure 1324-avoidance in the tail. A 1324 beginning at $X$ would need three larger successors. The first deletion succeeds. Deleting $Z$ leaves the maximum at the first position. Deleting the last position leaves a connected final block with a point below $X$; all preceding tail points are adjacent to $X$ in the inversion graph. These two deletions therefore leave the input indecomposable, as required for a late orientation. In the constructions with final block $1\ominus T$ we have $|T|\ge2$, so both sides of that skew block survive deletion of its last point. With final block 321 the surviving block is 21.

- If $c(a)\ge3$, write $a=S\oplus I_j\oplus T$ with $j\ge1$ and indecomposable ends. When $|T|\ge2$ take tail $S\oplus I_{j-1}\oplus(1\ominus T)$. The input has length $h$ and its tail has one fewer component than $a$, so it avoids $q$. Grow the middle and discard $Z$ to obtain $q$. When $T=1$, use $S\oplus I_{j-1}\oplus321$ instead. Now $q$ ends at its maximum. An occurrence using $X$ would have to end at $Z$, which has two later points in an input of length $h+1$. Omitting $X$ leaves the entire tail, whose maximum is before its end. In the image choose $X,S,I_j$ and the second entry of the final 321.
- If $a=A\oplus T$ has two components and $T$ starts below its maximum, take tail $A\oplus(1\ominus T)$. The maximum of $q$ is inside $T$, away from its last position because $T$ is indecomposable. Thus $q$ is indecomposable. Omitting $X$ leaves a decomposable tail of length $h$. Using $X$ would require $Z$ at the position of the maximum of $q$. Its position in the entire input is $|A|+2$, while that maximum has position at least $|A|+3$ in $q$. After growth select $X,A,T$, dropping the middle point and $Z$.
- If $a=T$ is indecomposable, avoids 213 and starts below its maximum, take tail $1\oplus(1\ominus T)$. Once again $q$ is indecomposable. An occurrence omitting $X$ would have to fill the entire last block, which starts at its maximum. In an occurrence using $X$ the indecomposable $T$ must lie wholly in the last block. It would have to select $Z$, its first point, for its maximum, contrary to its first entry. After growth select $X,T$.

In every construction the prescribed map changes which side of $X$ contains the largest value below $Z$ in the last component. This gives precisely the required second-largest first rank in the selected image. Undoing the indexed symmetry proves Test 3 and the classification. ∎

The exact numbers of compatible members of $\operatorname{Av}_h(1324)$ for $h=4,\ldots,10$ are $3,12,66,675,6599,55875,431491$. These are the values previously reported by the source as its computational upper bounds. The characterization applies at every length. Compatibility implies inversion monotonicity in the region $k\le2n-7$ by the source injection. Their separately numbered Problem 8.1 asks for monotonicity at all $n,k$ for some four-letter partner.
