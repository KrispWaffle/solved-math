# Derangement limits in every polynomial-growth permutation class

**Theorem.** Let $\mathcal P$ be any infinite hereditary permutation class with $|\mathcal P_n|$ bounded by a polynomial in $n$. The derangement proportions at even lengths and at odd lengths each converge to a rational number. A finite calculation on the class’s peg-permutation description gives the two numbers. The full sequence converges exactly when they agree. Its attainable limits, as the polynomial-growth class varies, are exactly the rational numbers in $[0,1]$. If the class has bounded enumeration, the attainable limits are the rationals in $[0,1)$.

This gives a convergence criterion for the polynomial-growth part of Vatter’s numbered Question 4.3, alongside the full unrestricted answer to his attainable-values question. The following description also gives the fixed-point distribution on its finite and linear scales. [Vatter, §4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16355v4#page=18) · [Unrestricted spectrum](PROOF.md)

## Disjoint enumeration by block lengths

A peg permutation is a finite permutation with each entry marked as increasing, decreasing or a single point. Its inflation replaces each entry by a block of consecutive positions and consecutive values in the prescribed order. Homberger and Vatter’s Theorem 1.4 encodes all members of $\mathcal P$ **uniquely** by a finite collection of peg permutations with specified sets of integer block lengths. Those sets are intersections of upsets and downsets for coordinatewise comparison. Membership is a Boolean combination of finitely many coordinatewise lower bounds, by the finiteness of minimal vectors in each upset. [Homberger–Vatter, Theorem 1.4 and §2.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1308.4946#page=4)

In each coordinate separate every integer value up to the largest threshold from the single infinite interval above it. Keep the resulting Cartesian boxes belonging to the length set. For a given box let $d$ be the number of unbounded coordinates, write their lengths as $b_i+t_i$ with $t_i\ge0$, and let $c$ be the sum of all fixed lengths and lower bounds. Length $n$ means $\sum t_i=n-c$. A box with $d\ge1$ contributes $\binom{n-c+d-1}{d-1}$ members for $n\ge c$. A box with no unbounded coordinates contributes at one length. Let $D$ be the maximum $d$. Infinitude ensures $D\ge1$. The boxes with $d=D$ all have the same leading count $n^{D-1}/(D-1)!$, and the remaining boxes have asymptotically zero weight. Thus at large lengths one can first choose a maximum-dimensional box with equal limiting probabilities, then choose a uniform weak composition in its $D$ coordinates.

## Fixed points in a single block

For a block of positive length $h$ let $u$ be the number of entries before its positions and $v$ the number below its values. Both are sums of entire block lengths, determined by the peg. With indices starting at zero, a local position $j$ has global position $u+j$. Its value is $v+j$ for an increasing block and $v+h-1-j$ for a decreasing block. A dotted entry may be treated as an increasing block of length one. Hence an increasing block contributes exactly $h$ fixed points when $u=v$, and zero otherwise. A decreasing block contributes one exactly when
```math
-(h-1)\le v-u\le h-1,\qquad v-u+h-1\equiv 0\pmod2.
\tag{1}
```
It contributes zero in every other case. This includes blocks of length one in either orientation.

## The two rational parity limits

Fix a maximum-dimensional box, a parity of $n$, and the parity vector $e_i=t_i\bmod2$. Exactly $2^{D-1}$ vectors are compatible with $\sum_i e_i\equiv n-c\pmod2$. For each one, putting $t_i=2s_i+e_i$ leaves an ordinary uniform weak composition of $(n-c-\sum e_i)/2$. Its count divided by the full box count tends to $2^{1-D}$. This also holds for $D=1$, when one vector is possible. The parity tests in (1) are now constants.

Set $N=n-c$ and $x_i=t_i/N$ at sufficiently large lengths. Conditional on the parity vector, $x$ converges to the uniform probability measure on the simplex $\Delta=\{x_i\ge0,\sum x_i=1\}$. This is the ordinary Riemann-sum limit on the regular mesh of weak compositions in the first $D-1$ coordinates. The parity and lower-bound offsets shift this mesh by $O(1/N)$. For $D=1$ take the point mass at $x_1=1$.

All remaining comparisons in the block test have the form $N L(x)+b$ with $L$ a rational linear form and $b$ a fixed integer. When $L$ is identically zero on $\Delta$, use the exact test on $b$ at every length. When $L$ is constant and nonzero, its sign eventually decides the test. Otherwise its zero hyperplane has relative measure zero. Away from those finitely many hyperplanes, every comparison eventually has the sign of its leading form. This holds uniformly on the complement of any fixed neighborhoods of the hyperplanes, whose volumes tend to zero as the neighborhoods shrink. It applies to equalities as well as strict and weak inequalities. For $D=1$ all forms are constant on the simplex.

The derangement test is therefore a union of cells in a finite rational hyperplane arrangement in $\Delta$, with the constant and parity tests retained. Its relative volume is rational: in the first $D-1$ coordinates the closed cells have rational vertices and admit triangulations into rational simplices. Boundaries of lower dimension do not affect the answer. Taking the equal average over compatible parity vectors and maximum-dimensional boxes gives the stated two rational numbers and the equality criterion.

The same partition lets one compute the limiting probability of every fixed finite number of fixed points along each parity. A free increasing block whose equality $u=v$ holds identically has a length tending to infinity in probability. In boxes containing such a block the fixed-point count escapes to infinity. In every other box the count, outside an event of probability tending to zero, comes from the fixed-length blocks and the at most one hit per decreasing block. The limiting law in that box is supported on finitely many integers, and each mass is a rational sum of cell volumes. On the one-point compactification of the nonnegative integers this gives weak convergence on both parities. The probability at infinity is the fraction of maximum-dimensional boxes with at least one identically aligned free increasing block, the same for both parities.

At scale $n$, the fraction of fixed points has a limit along the **entire sequence**. In each box keep only its increasing free coordinates for which $u=v$ holds identically on the box. Every other increasing free coordinate has a fixed point only on an event of probability tending to zero, and all other contributions are bounded. If exactly $a$ of the $D$ free coordinates are kept, the limit there is the sum of those $a$ coordinates of a uniform point of $\Delta$. It is zero for $a=0$, one for $a=D$, and has the $\mathrm{Beta}(a,D-a)$ law otherwise. The beta density follows by taking the product of the two simplex-slice volumes at a prescribed sum $t$, proportional to $t^{a-1}(1-t)^{D-a-1}$. The class’s limit is the equal mixture over the maximum-dimensional boxes. This law is independent of the parity vector.

## Realizing every rational

For integers $0\le a<b$, let $I$ be the increasing class and let $D_{\le k}$ consist of decreasing permutations of length at most $k$, including the empty permutation. Consider the hereditary union
```math
(I\ominus D_{\le a})\ \cup\ (I\oplus D_{\le b-a}).
\tag{2}
```
For $n>2b$, the skew family gives the identity and $a$ derangements. The length of the final low decreasing tail is determined by the first value in each nonidentity member. Its initial increasing block shifts up by the tail length, while all values in the tail lie strictly below their positions. The direct family contributes the identity and $b-a-1$ more members with fixed points. Its tails of sizes zero and one give the same identity, and larger sizes are distinguished by their last value. All its members begin with a fixed point. Thus the two families meet only at the identity, and the union has exactly $a$ derangements among exactly $b$ members at every sufficiently large length. The skew family in (2) is the published Zhang–Ma construction. [Prior family, Vatter’s final update](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16355v4#page=32)

The cyclic shifts of the increasing permutation form $I\ominus I$. Their count at length $n\ge1$ is $n$, with precisely $n-1$ derangements. This polynomial-growth class realizes one. No infinite class of bounded enumeration realizes one. By the Erdős–Szekeres theorem, arbitrarily long members of the class contain arbitrarily long increasing or arbitrarily long decreasing permutations. Heredity gives all lengths in at least one such monotone family. At every odd length that family provides a permutation with a fixed point, a fraction bounded away from zero when the slice sizes are bounded. Alternatively the same unbounded monotone block is visible in the peg description. This proves both spectrum statements. ∎


## Known cases and formal verification

Albert, Bouvel and Féray characterize classes where the fixed-point predicate is first-order definable in the theory of two orders. For polynomial-growth classes satisfying that condition, the first-order convergence theorem of Falgas-Ravry, Markström, Räty and Skerman already gives full-sequence rational limits for every fixed finite count. Elizalde gives exact distributions in simultaneous length-three avoidance classes. Janson’s three-block Dirichlet law for $\operatorname{Av}(132,321)$ already yields the particular $\mathrm{Beta}(1,2)$ fixed-point fraction. Woods’s theorem on Presburger counting also gives eventual quasipolynomial enumeration for each fixed count after encoding the block conditions. The parity proof above identifies just two leading limits and gives the general law on both scales. [Definability, Theorem 33](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.05459#page=29) · [First-order convergence, Theorem 1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.34328#page=5) · [Elizalde](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0311211) · [Janson, §7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.06071#page=14) · [Woods](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.0020)

Lean proves both fixed-point formulas on actual monotone blocks. It constructs the hereditary class in (2), proves its exact counts at all $n>2b$ and derives attainment of every rational $a/b$ with $0\le a<b$. The cyclic-shift endpoint is also fully formalized. The peg decomposition, rational-volume computation and mixture law have written proofs.
